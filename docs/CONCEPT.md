# Concept

## Problem statement

Fusion needs tungsten, lithium (lithium-6), beryllium and rare earths. These materials already sit in supply chains linked to conflict finance in eastern DR Congo, child labour reports at Rubaya and poisoned water in Kachin State, Myanmar. Supply is highly concentrated: China accounts for about 79% of tungsten mining and around 80% of tungsten APT and rare earth refining. Lithium-6 has almost no commercial supply.

The project frames this as an anticipatory governance problem. Fusion is still early enough that sourcing rules can be designed before procurement locks in the next phase of risk.

## Thesis: anticipatory governance

Fusion is not yet commercial, so its supply chain is still small enough to shape. Unlike cobalt and EV batteries, responsible sourcing can be built in before demand ramps up. The window closes when the first commercial plants are procured.

This gives the project a practical aim: to show how supply chain risk, human-rights risk and the security logic around critical minerals can be aligned before scale-up.

## Three angles

### The AI–fusion feedback loop

Big Tech buys fusion power. Google has a 200 MW deal with CFS and Microsoft has a 50 MW deal with Helion by 2028. That makes AI demand part of the fusion materials story.

### Supply security vs. human rights

Governments often present fusion materials as a security issue. The site shows that security and human-rights concerns are linked, not separate: concentrated supply, export controls and fragile traceability are both operational and social risks.

### Learning from batteries

The battery sector offers a warning. Due diligence often arrives after demand is large, and the result is avoidance rather than remedy. Fusion has a narrower window to avoid that pattern.

## Audiences and what each needs

- Fusion investors and power buyers: materials risk as project risk; term-sheet language.
- EU policymakers: instrument-by-instrument amendments.
- UN Task Force on Critical Energy Transition Minerals: scope language for traceability and accountability.
- Secondary audiences: NGOs, journalists, researchers and students.

## Core argument: remedy first, exit last

Contract clauses alone create “ditch and run.” Remedy happens only when it is required, measured and funded.

The project argues that buyers should engage suppliers, require corrective action plans, fund remedy and suspend supply only when milestones are missed. Exit is a last resort, not the default response.

## Design principles

- Evidence first.
- Gaps are shown as gaps.
- Exposure is not wrongdoing.
- Every claim one click from its source.
- Briefs stand alone without interaction.
- Restraint over drama.

## Visual identity

The site uses a graphite palette with gold accents for structure and data, and rust for harm to people. Mineral colours follow ore types: tungsten in blue-grey, lithium in lilac, beryllium in green and rare earths in ochre. The typography uses Newsreader for headings, Geist for body text and Geist Mono for labels and data. The timeline is an abstract transit-style rail with a single fork and no decorative vehicles.

## Decisions log

- No 3D reactor model; use an exploded 2D cutaway instead.
- No policy lever map; each brief carries its own levers.
- No money-flow diagram; the chain from Big Tech to armed groups cannot be evidenced.
- Separate pages per audience rather than a view toggle.
- Concentration bars and small locators instead of a world globe.
- Harm hotspots sit beside concentration bars because the most concentrated country is often not where harm happens.
- Removed the “security lens vs people lens” section because it was not part of the agreed concept.

## Summary

The concept is a research site that makes the material risk behind fusion visible before scale-up. It focuses on concentration, conflict exposure, governance gaps and the need to design remedy into contracts before the market hardens.
