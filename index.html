Fusion energy · supply chains · human rights

Fusion could provide low-carbon power. Its material supply chains still warrant scrutiny.

Fusion reactor designs may rely on tungsten, lithium-6, beryllium and rare earths. Their supply chains vary in concentration, traceability and documented social or environmental risk. This research maps those exposure points without inferring that a named fusion company has sourced from them.

Glossary

CAHRA

Conflict-affected and high-risk area.

Tokamak

A magnetic confinement device designed to hold a hot plasma long enough for fusion to happen.

Stellarator

An alternative magnetic confinement design that uses twisted field geometry rather than a toroidal tokamak arrangement.

Power purchase agreement

A contract in which a buyer agrees to purchase electricity from a project, often for a fixed term.

Remedy

Measures to address harm, including corrective action, support and just transition for affected workers and communities.

3T

Tin, tungsten and tantalum, the minerals often covered by conflict-minerals due diligence rules.

APT

Ammonium paratungstate, a commonly traded tungsten intermediate.

Rare earths

Minerals used in magnets and components, including heavy and light rare earths.

What is fusion

Fusion

Two small nuclei merge. Helium and a neutron are produced.

Fission

A large nucleus splits into smaller parts.

In the deuterium-tritium reaction used in many magnetic-confinement designs, light nuclei fuse at temperatures on the order of 150 million °C, producing helium, a neutron and energy. Fission instead splits heavy nuclei. Other fusion concepts may use different fuel cycles and operating conditions.

  • The fusion reaction itself does not emit carbon dioxide; the full lifecycle footprint also depends on construction, materials and energy inputs.
  • Waste characteristics differ from fission and depend on material choices and neutron exposure.
  • Commercial, sustained electricity production has not yet been demonstrated.

Why now

1. Procurement choices are still developing

Many fusion projects have not reached commercial-scale procurement, leaving an opportunity to ask how traceability and remedy could be incorporated into purchasing decisions.

2. Some supply stages are concentrated

Available estimates indicate substantial concentration in parts of tungsten and rare-earth supply chains. The figures refer to different materials and processing stages and should not be read as a single market measure.

3. Early requirements can shape practice

Contractual and reporting expectations established before large-scale procurement may be easier to implement than requirements introduced after supply relationships are established.

What the gaps mean

The instruments summarized here do not create a fusion-specific due-diligence regime for lithium-6, beryllium or rare earths. Their general or product-specific requirements may still apply depending on the company, activity and market. The EU Conflict Minerals Regulation focuses on specified 3TG imports and does not cover every downstream product or component. These limits leave questions about how emerging fusion supply chains will be addressed; they do not mean that no relevant law or guidance applies.

Evidence: demand

Published estimates cited in this project put lithium-6 requirements for some pilot-plant blanket concepts in the tens-of-tonnes range. Commercial availability at that scale is not established. Historical production at Oak Ridge’s Y-12 plant is not a measure of present-day commercial supply.

Known and pending

Lithium-6

Needed per pilot plant: order of tens of tonnes.

Commercial supply at fusion-relevant scale: not established.

Tungsten

It is a major structural material for the first wall and divertor, but plant-level tonnage estimates remain highly design-specific.

Public reporting is stronger on concentration risk than on the exact per-reactor material bill.

Beryllium

Design choices vary across blanket and multiplier concepts, so the tonnage picture is still preliminary and architecture-dependent.

The key issue is not just supply volume; it is the narrowness of public, fusion-specific procurement visibility.

Rare earths

High-field magnets can rely on rare-earth-containing superconducting technologies, but plant demand remains uncertain across reactor designs.

That uncertainty matters because refining concentration is already one of the most visible bottlenecks in the market.

Remedy-first framework

Where a supplier relationship can responsibly continue, an identified risk should trigger assessment and corrective action rather than automatic disengagement. Severe impacts may require suspension or termination.

Identify

Mapping or audit finds a risk. The first response is to understand the exposure and agree what evidence is missing.

Three guardrails

  • No blanket regional exclusion.
  • Report outcomes, not just avoidance.
  • Fund remedy.

Why it matters: the ditch-and-run problem

A September 2026 Reuters report on forced-labour enforcement describes persistent shortcomings in social audits, including concerns about audit duration, worker participation and the possibility that assessments can be managed by the audited company. It also reports an estimate of 27.6 million people in forced labour and discusses the EU’s product-ban approach. These are claims reported in that article, not findings specific to fusion supply chains.

The report is relevant here as a governance analogy, not proof that market-access penalties will work in every sector. If buyers respond to risk only by switching suppliers, they may transfer the costs to workers and communities while leaving the underlying conditions unaddressed. A responsible response should combine credible evidence, worker-informed assessment, time-bound corrective action and remedy; suspension or exit may be necessary when severe harm persists or engagement cannot reduce it.

Reuters also cites a study by the Global Accountability Project that found a 22-percentage-point reduction in audit failures when businesses faced a threat of lost business. That result supports examining the role of commercial consequences, but it does not establish a causal effect for fusion procurement or demonstrate that product bans alone deliver remedy.

What happens if…

Audit finds child labour

Escalate through the ladder: engage, establish a corrective action plan and support monitoring.

Smelter cannot document origin

Pause new orders while engagement continues and insist on traceability evidence.

Supplier is financing armed groups

Immediate exit with notice and a transition period, while assessing the impact on workers.

Materials risk is project risk. Manage it before it delays your plant.

Ask: Require a remedy-first sourcing policy in every fusion-offtake and project-finance contract.

  1. Map tungsten, lithium-6, beryllium and rare earths to smelter level before major procurement.
  2. Put remedy-first escalation in the term sheet.
  3. Report outcomes and supplier milestones every year.

Why now: many projects remain pre-commercial, so procurement expectations can be considered while designs, financing and supplier relationships are still developing.

Address fusion materials in EU due-diligence policy before procurement scales.

Ask: Add fusion materials to the due-diligence and funding conditions now, before major EU-backed projects lock in procurement.

  1. Condition public funding on OECD-aligned due diligence and reporting.
  2. Review the Conflict Minerals Regulation and name fusion-related materials.
  3. Require remedy-first clauses in Fusion for Energy procurement and licensing rules.

Why now: Euratom 2026–27 committed €222 million to fusion, and the sector is still early enough to align standards before supplier relationships harden.

Why it matters to you

Fusion could contribute to lower-carbon energy systems, but that potential does not remove the need to examine material sourcing. This brief asks whether EU funding and procurement mechanisms can encourage proportionate due diligence and remediation without implying that EU fusion projects currently use conflict-affected sources.

Actions in detail

Funding condition

“EU funding for fusion projects shall require OECD-aligned due diligence for tungsten, lithium-6, beryllium and rare earths; a grievance mechanism; a remedy-first escalation ladder; and annual reporting on corrective actions and remediation outcomes.”

Regulation review

“The EU should commit in the 2026–27 review of the Conflict Minerals Regulation to extend coverage beyond raw tungsten to tungsten components and assess beryllium and rare earths in fusion supply chains, while making clear that blanket avoidance is not compliance.”

Licensing condition

“The EU should model a fusion licensing rule on the Batteries Regulation: a verified due diligence policy as a condition of permission, remedy-first escalation as a binding requirement, and public reporting on what was remediated and what remains unresolved.”

Review the governance gaps.

Limits and next steps: This brief does not claim that EU fusion projects source conflict-affected minerals. Its recommendations are policy proposals, not descriptions of current legal requirements. Their relevance should be assessed against applicable law and any subsequent amendments.

Consider fusion materials in critical-minerals traceability discussions.

Ask: Add fusion-relevant materials to the UN traceability and accountability agenda and embed remedy-first principles before the market scales.

  1. Add tungsten, lithium-6, beryllium and rare earths to the task-force scope.
  2. Adopt remedy-first as a principle, not just disclosure of risk.

Why now: UN critical-energy-transition-minerals work offers a forum to examine how traceability and accountability approaches relate to emerging energy technologies, including fusion.

Why it matters to you

Fusion-specific material requirements and supply-chain evidence remain limited in the public record. The task force can clarify where existing critical-minerals work applies and where further analysis may be useful.

Actions in detail

Scope language

“The framework should explicitly include fusion-relevant minerals, including tungsten, lithium-6, beryllium and rare earths, and should treat them as materials of concern where concentrated supply chains, traceability gaps or human-rights risk intersect with emerging energy technology demand.”

Remedy-first language

“The framework should require a remedy-first approach: engagement, corrective action, measurable milestones, support for affected communities and workers, and a disciplined exit only after earlier steps fail or where the abuse is most severe.”

Other relevant forums include UNEA, the Security Council and the UN Forum on Business and Human Rights in Geneva.

Limits and next steps: This brief does not claim direct use of conflict-sourced material by named fusion companies. The next decision point is the UN Task Force analytical study and expert discussions in 2027.

Methodology

Research questions: Which stages of selected fusion-material supply chains are concentrated or difficult to trace? What social and environmental risks are reported in relevant producing regions? How do existing due-diligence instruments apply to those materials and downstream products?

Scope: five materials — tungsten, lithium, lithium-6, beryllium and rare earths — considered across five stages: mine, trade, refining, components and plant. Lithium and lithium-6 are treated separately where isotopic separation changes the supply-chain analysis.

Sources and tools: The research draws on USGS commodity information, UN materials, NGO and investigative reporting, policy instruments, industry publications and public company disclosures. Sayari and web-search tools were used for discovery and supply-chain research; they do not independently verify every relationship presented.

Evidence handling: Claims are categorized as documented, reported or inferred. “Documented” indicates support in a primary record or directly available data; “reported” identifies claims attributed to an external source; “inferred” marks the project’s interpretation of available evidence. These labels describe evidentiary status, not the severity of a risk.

Naming policy: A location-level or material-level risk is not evidence that a named fusion company is exposed to a specific source. Company names are included for sector context only; the site does not allege that any named company has used conflict-sourced material.

AI-use disclosure: AI tools assisted with research and drafting. The author is responsible for reviewing the content and should verify claims against the cited source documents before relying on them.

Limitations

Public information on fusion-specific material demand and procurement is limited. Estimates for lithium-6, beryllium, tungsten and rare earths depend on reactor design, material substitutions and system boundaries; this site does not provide a complete bill of materials or a plant-level demand forecast.

Supply-chain concentration and reports of harm in a producing region do not establish that a particular fusion project purchases from that region. Publicly available trade and company data may also be aggregated, incomplete or difficult to connect to specific components.

Legal coverage depends on the instrument’s scope, the product, the company and the relevant jurisdiction. The governance matrix is a high-level comparison, not legal advice. Claims and estimates should be checked against the underlying source and its publication date.
