Fusion energy · supply chains · human rights

Fusion will run on minerals from some of the world’s most harmful supply chains. The rules for those chains are not yet written.

Leading fusion designs use tungsten, lithium-6, beryllium and rare earths. Their supply chains run through armed-group-controlled mines in eastern Congo and poisoned hillsides in Myanmar, and most refining sits in one country. This site maps where the risks are and what regulators can do before the first plants are built.

Glossary

CAHRA

Conflict-affected and high-risk area.

Tokamak

A magnetic confinement device designed to hold a hot plasma long enough for fusion to happen.

Stellarator

An alternative magnetic confinement design that uses twisted field geometry rather than a toroidal tokamak arrangement.

Power purchase agreement

A contract in which a buyer agrees to purchase electricity from a project, often for a fixed term.

Remedy

Measures to address harm, including corrective action, support and just transition for affected workers and communities.

3T

Tin, tungsten and tantalum, the minerals often covered by conflict-minerals due diligence rules.

APT

Ammonium paratungstate, a commonly traded tungsten intermediate.

Rare earths

Minerals used in magnets and components, including heavy and light rare earths.

What is fusion

Fusion

Two small nuclei merge. Helium and a neutron are produced.

Fission

A large nucleus splits into smaller parts.

In the deuterium-tritium reaction used in many magnetic-confinement designs, light nuclei fuse at temperatures on the order of 150 million °C, producing helium, a neutron and energy. Fission instead splits heavy nuclei. Other fusion concepts may use different fuel cycles and operating conditions.

  • The fusion reaction itself does not emit carbon dioxide; the full lifecycle footprint also depends on construction, materials and energy inputs.
  • Waste characteristics differ from fission and depend on material choices and neutron exposure.
  • Commercial, sustained electricity production has not yet been demonstrated.

Tokamak cross-section

Simplified tokamak. Not to scale. Other designs use materials differently.

Case files

Tungsten · 3TDocumented

Rubaya, North Kivu

DR Congo · controlled by M23 since 2024

Miners dig by hand for a few dollars a day. Two collapses in early 2026 were each reported to kill about 200 people; victims of the first included children and market women. M23 disputes the second toll. UN experts report that M23 runs a parallel administration over Rubaya, earns at least $800,000 a month from taxing coltan, and smuggles 3T minerals into Rwanda. Rubaya supplies more than 15% of the world’s tantalum.

Who is affected: Artisanal miners and their families; communities under armed-group administration.

Most evidence concerns coltan; tungsten volumes from Rubaya are not quantified.

Rare earthsReported

Kachin Special Region 1

Myanmar · territory of junta-aligned militias

Mining sites rose by more than 40% from 2021 to 2023, to over 300. Leaching chemicals are pumped into hillsides; Myanmar’s imports of ammonium sulphate from China reached 1.5 million tonnes in 2023, up from 93,000 tonnes in 2015. Streams test highly acidic with elevated arsenic. Workers report coughing, numbness, skin conditions and kidney problems, and two miners died after leaving work, one aged 15. Residents say militias use coercion and payoffs to take land for mining.

“There are no longer fish in the waters.” — resident of Chipwe

Who is affected: Miners, farming and fishing communities, and residents displaced from riverbanks.

Trace this stage →
LithiumReported

High Andean salt flats

Argentina, Bolivia and Chile

Brine extraction in open evaporation ponds causes an irreversible loss of water from aquifers. In Chile’s Salar de Atacama, groundwater has fallen by more than 10 metres in 15 years and the salt flat is sinking 1 to 2 cm a year. At least 200 Indigenous communities live around the salt flats; most lithium operations went ahead without free, prior and informed consent.

Who is affected: Indigenous communities whose water, grazing and livelihoods depend on the salt flats.

Fusion needs lithium enriched in lithium-6; the extraction stage is shared with battery lithium.

BerylliumDocumented

Beryllium workers

Beryllium processing and machining plants

Inhaling small beryllium particles can sensitise workers, and 6–8% of sensitised people develop chronic beryllium disease each year, a lung disease. At least 134,000 current US workers are estimated to be exposed, and sensitisation is found in 1–16% of exposed workers tested. Families of workers and people living near plants have also become ill.

Who is affected: Machinists, process and lab workers, and their families.

These figures are from the United States; the global beryllium chain is not yet mapped.

What we don’t know yet

  • No public data links harm to tungsten mining specifically; most Great Lakes evidence concerns coltan.
  • Mining communities in China, where most tungsten and rare earths are mined and refined, are not yet researched.
  • Beryllium mining and processing sites outside the US are not mapped.
  • No fusion-specific procurement data exists to trace any case to a fusion buyer.

Why now

1. First plants are about to be procured

SPARC targets first plasma in 2027 and the first commercial plants are due in the early 2030s. Sourcing set then will last decades.

2. Supply is already squeezed

China mines 79% of tungsten and refines about 80% of rare earths. Export licensing in 2025 doubled tungsten prices.

3. The rules are being written now

The EU is reviewing its conflict minerals law and amending the Critical Raw Materials Act; the US and UK are setting fusion regulations. None covers where fusion’s minerals come from.

What the gaps mean

No binding rule requires due diligence on lithium-6, beryllium or rare earths used in fusion. Tungsten is covered only as raw ore and metal, not inside finished parts. Fusion companies are too small for the CSDDD, and fusion is not among the strategic technologies whose large manufacturers must assess raw-material supply risks under the Critical Raw Materials Act.

Evidence: demand

Published estimates cited in this project put lithium-6 requirements for some pilot-plant blanket concepts in the tens-of-tonnes range. Commercial availability at that scale is not established. Historical production at Oak Ridge’s Y-12 plant is not a measure of present-day commercial supply.

Known and pending

Lithium-6

Needed per pilot plant: order of tens of tonnes.

Commercial supply at fusion-relevant scale: not established.

Tungsten

It is a major structural material for the first wall and divertor, but plant-level tonnage estimates remain highly design-specific.

Public reporting is stronger on concentration risk than on the exact per-reactor material bill.

Beryllium

Design choices vary across blanket and multiplier concepts, so the tonnage picture is still preliminary and architecture-dependent.

The key issue is not just supply volume; it is the narrowness of public, fusion-specific procurement visibility.

Rare earths

High-field magnets can rely on rare-earth-containing superconducting technologies, but plant demand remains uncertain across reactor designs.

That uncertainty matters because refining concentration is already one of the most visible bottlenecks in the market.

Remedy-first framework

When a risk is found, buyers should fix it, not flee it. Exit is the last step, and immediate only for the gravest abuses.

Identify

Mapping or audit finds a risk. The first response is to understand the exposure and agree what evidence is missing.

Three guardrails

  • No blanket regional exclusion.
  • Report outcomes, not just avoidance.
  • Fund remedy.

Why it matters: the ditch-and-run problem

After the US conflict minerals rule took effect, many buyers avoided eastern Congo instead of cleaning up supply. Research found looting, battles and violence against civilians rose in affected areas, especially around gold mines. Fusion buyers need small volumes and can switch origins easily, so avoidance is the cheapest way to comply unless rules require remedy.

What happens if…

Audit finds child labour

Escalate through the ladder: engage, establish a corrective action plan and support monitoring.

Smelter cannot document origin

Pause new orders while engagement continues and insist on traceability evidence.

Supplier is financing armed groups

Immediate exit with notice and a transition period, while assessing the impact on workers.

This framework is built into all three briefs.

Materials risk is project risk. Manage it before it delays your plant.

Ask: Require a remedy-first sourcing policy in every fusion-offtake and project-finance contract.

  1. Map tungsten, lithium-6, beryllium and rare earths to smelter level before major procurement.
  2. Put remedy-first escalation in the term sheet.
  3. Report outcomes and supplier milestones every year.

Why now: First-plant supply contracts will be signed in the next two to three years. Terms set now are cheap; retrofits are not.

Make fusion the first EU energy technology with responsible sourcing built in.

Ask: Add fusion materials to the due-diligence and funding conditions now, before major EU-backed projects lock in procurement.

  1. Set OECD-aligned conditions on EU fusion funding and Fusion for Energy procurement now.
  2. Add fusion equipment to the CRMA business risk-preparedness list in the current amendments.
  3. Extend the Conflict Minerals Regulation to tungsten components and assess beryllium and rare earths in its review.
  4. Signal a fusion-specific standard in the EU Fusion Strategy and national licensing.

Why now: Euratom 2026–27 committed €222 million to fusion, and the sector is still early enough to align standards before supplier relationships harden.

Why now

Why it matters to you

The EU funds fusion to cut energy dependence. If fusion’s minerals come from one dominant supplier or from conflict zones, it rebuilds the dependence it was meant to end. No EU rule closes that gap today.

Actions in detail

Funding condition

“EU funding for fusion projects shall require OECD-aligned due diligence for tungsten, lithium-6, beryllium and rare earths; a grievance mechanism; a remedy-first escalation ladder; and annual reporting on corrective actions and remediation outcomes.”

Regulation review

“The EU should commit in the 2026–27 review of the Conflict Minerals Regulation to extend coverage beyond raw tungsten to tungsten components and assess beryllium and rare earths in fusion supply chains, while making clear that blanket avoidance is not compliance.”

Fusion Strategy commitment

“The EU Fusion Strategy should commit to OECD-aligned due diligence for tungsten, lithium-6, beryllium and rare earths across EU-funded and EU-procured fusion projects, require remedy before exit, and recommend that national fusion regulators adopt the same standard.”

Review the governance gaps.

Limits: These are proposals, not current law. No EU fusion project is alleged to use conflict-sourced minerals.

Methodology

Research questions: Which stages of selected fusion-material supply chains are concentrated or difficult to trace? What social and environmental risks are reported in relevant producing regions? How do existing due-diligence instruments apply to those materials and downstream products?

Scope: five materials — tungsten, lithium, lithium-6, beryllium and rare earths — considered across five stages: mine, trade, refining, components and plant. Lithium and lithium-6 are treated separately where isotopic separation changes the supply-chain analysis.

Sources and tools: The evidence base combines official data, law, UN material, NGO and investigative reporting, research and public industry information. See the linked APA references →

Evidence handling: Claims are categorized as documented, reported or inferred. “Documented” indicates support in a primary record or directly available data; “reported” identifies claims attributed to an external source; “inferred” marks the project’s interpretation of available evidence. These labels describe evidentiary status, not the severity of a risk.

AI-use disclosure: AI tools assisted with research, coding and drafting.

Limitations

Public information on fusion-specific material demand and procurement is limited. Estimates for lithium-6, beryllium, tungsten and rare earths depend on reactor design, material substitutions and system boundaries; this site does not provide a complete bill of materials or a plant-level demand forecast.

Publicly available trade and company data may be aggregated, incomplete or difficult to connect to specific components. Regional evidence describes exposure in shared supply chains, not the sourcing practices of a particular fusion project.

Legal coverage depends on the instrument’s scope, the product, the company and the relevant jurisdiction. The governance matrix is a high-level comparison, not legal advice. Claims and estimates should be checked against the underlying source and its publication date.
